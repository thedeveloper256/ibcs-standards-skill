# CONDENSE — Increase Information Density

CONDENSE covers all aspects of increasing information density in reporting. Increasing information density means including all information necessary to understand the situation or key message on one page. A consistent semantic notation paves the way for highly condensed reporting.

This chapter covers using small components, utilizing space, and adding data, components, and visuals.

**Source:** IBCS v2.0, pages 143–156.

## CO 1 — Use small elements

The need for higher information density requires displaying visuals, components, and signs as small as possible while still legible. Different technical parameters apply to print, screens, and projected slides.

### CO 1.1 — Use small fonts

**Requirement.** Avoid oversize fonts.

**Why.** Oversize fonts needlessly waste space that could carry additional information.

**Apply.** Use the smallest font size that remains legible for the medium. Keep type hierarchy consistent; do not enlarge text merely to fill space.

**Exceptions / limits.** Titles, key messages, and headings may need larger type than body/label text, but the hierarchy should still be compact.

**Audit.** Is any font larger than necessary for legibility and hierarchy?

**See also.** CO 1.2, CO 1.3.

**Source.** p. 143.

### CO 1.2 — Use small components

**Requirement.** Use small visual components.

**Why.** Small components increase clarity; large components are not easier to understand and consume space.

**Apply.** Reduce the size of bars, columns, markers, and labels to the minimum that remains legible. Keep proportions and semantic widths intact.

**Exceptions / limits.** Do not shrink a component so far that its labels, widths, or contrasts become ambiguous.

**Audit.** Are components as small as legibility allows?

**See also.** CO 1.1, CO 1.3.

**Source.** p. 143–144.

### CO 1.3 — Use small visuals

**Requirement.** Size charts and tables as small as possible while keeping every detail and label readable.

**Why.** Smaller visuals leave room for more information and more context, improving understanding.

**Apply.** Start with the smallest legible size, then enlarge only where the data or labels require it. Use the freed space for related data, comparisons, or explanation.

**Exceptions / limits.** A chart may need to be larger when it carries many labels or when a small multiple must be readable at print size.

**Audit.** Is each visual as small as it can be without losing legibility?

**See also.** CO 1.1, CO 2.2, CO 5.1.

**Source.** p. 144.

## CO 2 — Maximize use of space

Utilizing free space is the fastest and easiest way to increase information density. Make better use of needlessly wide margins, frames, blank pages, or little-used pages by filling them with helpful context.

### CO 2.1 — Use narrow page margins

**Requirement.** Use narrow page margins rather than margins driven by corporate design or decoration.

**Why.** Extra-wide margins sacrifice valuable space that could carry information.

**Apply.** Reduce margins to the minimum that preserves printability, binding, and readability. Keep the layout consistent across pages.

**Exceptions / limits.** Binding, printer limits, and accessibility may require larger margins; document the reason.

**Audit.** Are the margins wider than necessary for production and readability?

**See also.** CO 2.2, SI 1.1.

**Source.** p. 144–145.

### CO 2.2 — Reduce empty space

**Requirement.** Reduce empty space in page layouts and in the layouts of charts and tables.

**Why.** Empty space lowers information density and can make a page look unfinished or poorly composed.

**Apply.** Tighten gaps, remove unused panels, and place related content where it is useful. However, retain some whitespace where it provides clarity, structure, and focus.

**Exceptions / limits.** Whitespace that separates groups, improves focus, or gives the eye rest is not waste. Do not remove it mechanically.

**Audit.** Is the empty space doing useful structural work, or is it merely unused?

**See also.** CO 2.1, SI 1.1.

**Source.** p. 145.

## CO 3 — Add data

Adding more data to an existing visualization increases information density and helps the reader understand the context.

### CO 3.1 — Add data points

**Requirement.** Display more data points where they help reveal relationships.

**Why.** Additional data points rarely jeopardize comprehension of numerical data and often reveal patterns that a short series hides.

**Apply.** Extend the time series, add comparison periods, or add related series. A monthly staff statistic over 36 months can be understood about as quickly as one over 12 months, and the longer series may reveal seasonality or trend changes.

**Exceptions / limits.** More points can overwhelm a chart with labels. Use selective labels and a suitable chart type (for example, a line chart) rather than shrinking the data.

**Audit.** Does the visual include enough data points to reveal the relevant pattern?

**See also.** CO 3.2, EX 1.1.

**Source.** p. 145–146.

### CO 3.2 — Add dimensions

**Requirement.** Show more than two dimensions of a business situation where useful.

**Why.** A one-dimensional chart often states only a mundane fact. Two dimensions can reveal relationships; three or more can reveal structures and new insights.

**Apply.** Add structure, scenario, time, or a third measure. Use grouped, stacked, multi-tier, or bubble charts as appropriate, and keep the notation consistent.

**Exceptions / limits.** More dimensions increase complexity; add only dimensions that serve the question and remain legible.

**Audit.** Does the visual show the dimensions needed to answer the question?

**See also.** CO 3.1, CO 4, EX 5.2.

**Source.** p. 146.

## CO 4 — Add visual components

Two or more basic chart types can be combined into a combined chart with higher information density. Combined charts are treated as one entity. There are three types: overlay, multi-tier, and extended charts. Chart components can also be embedded in tables, and explanations can be integrated.

### CO 4.1 — Show overlay charts

**Requirement.** Use overlay charts when two or more basic charts with the same category axis should be combined.

**Why.** Overlaying a line on columns, for example, can show two related measures in one compact visual.

**Apply.** Keep the category axis shared. Use different value axes only when necessary and label them clearly. Typical uses include sales columns with a return-on-sales line, columns with an employees line, or columns with integrated variance bars.

**Exceptions / limits.** Overlay charts with different value axes can be hard to read; integrated variance overlays make it difficult to label both series and to see the primary chart's development. If there is enough space, prefer a multi-tier chart.

**Audit.** Do the overlaid charts share a category axis and remain legible? Would a multi-tier chart be clearer?

**See also.** CO 4.2, UN 5.2.

**Source.** p. 147–149.

### CO 4.2 — Show multi-tier charts

**Requirement.** Use multi-tier charts to add tiers along the same category axis for analyses on the same basic data.

**Why.** A second or third tier can show absolute and relative variances next to the primary values without cramming them into the same plot.

**Apply.** For horizontal charts, place the secondary chart above the primary; for vertical charts, place it to the right. Reduplicate the category axis in the secondary chart, with the appropriate scenario/axis design. Give each tier its own value axis, but scale axes with the same unit identically. Add a third tier when needed (for example, grouped AC/PL columns, then absolute variances, then relative variance pins).

**Exceptions / limits.** Multi-tier charts use more space than overlays; they are the better choice when space allows and when labeling or reading the primary chart would otherwise suffer. More than three tiers is possible but special.

**Audit.** Are the tiers aligned on the same category axis and scaled correctly?

**See also.** CO 4.1, CO 4.3, UN 4.1.

**Source.** p. 149–150.

### CO 4.3 — Show extended charts

**Requirement.** Use extended charts to add context charts next to the primary chart by virtually extending the category axis.

**Why.** Context such as market averages, competitor figures, or a different perspective can be shown without a separate page.

**Apply.** For horizontal charts, place additional charts to the left or right; for vertical charts, above or below. Align their category axes with the virtual extension of the primary axis. Use the same value axis for the primary and additional charts.

**Exceptions / limits.** Extended charts are for context, not for unrelated measures. Keep the scale identical.

**Audit.** Are the additional charts aligned on the extended category axis and scaled identically?

**See also.** CO 4.2, CO 5.2.

**Source.** p. 151.

### CO 4.4 — Embed chart components in tables

**Requirement.** Increase table density with chart components such as table bars, sparklines, and traffic lights, where they add information.

**Why.** A table of numbers can be slow to scan; embedded components show magnitude and pattern directly.

**Apply.** Use table bars whose categories correspond to table rows; single bars, pins, and waterfall bars are suitable. Use sparklines only with proper scaling (for example, indexed), because individually scaled sparklines mislead. Use traffic lights only for two or three states (for example, yes/no).

**Exceptions / limits.** Omit sparklines if they cannot be scaled properly. Do not use traffic lights for magnitudes.

**Audit.** Do the embedded components share a correct scale and add information beyond the numbers?

**See also.** EX 2.5, CO 5.3.

**Source.** p. 152.

### CO 4.5 — Embed explanations

**Requirement.** Embed explanations into charts and tables where they refer directly to the visual.

**Why.** Embedded explanations increase both information density and comprehension by connecting the comment to the evidence.

**Apply.** Place short explanatory text next to the relevant component, linked with an annotation reference. In presentations, the speaker may provide the explanation instead.

**Exceptions / limits.** In live presentations, explanations are typically spoken; written reports, handouts, and dashboards benefit from embedded explanations.

**Audit.** Does the explanation sit next to the content it explains?

**See also.** UN 2.4, SA 4.5.

**Source.** p. 152–153.

## CO 5 — Add visuals

Reporting consists of one or more pages. The content of one page can be viewed together without flipping to other pages. Multiple visuals on one page increase density and comparability but demand a uniform notation and consistent scaling.

### CO 5.1 — Show small multiples

**Requirement.** Display charts of the same type and the same scale on the same page to compare multiple categories.

**Why.** Small multiples make complex relationships easier to grasp and allow comparison across countries, products, or projects.

**Apply.** Use the same chart type and identical scale for every panel. Up to around 25 charts is a practical limit; beyond that, add an “Others” chart that accumulates the remaining categories. If one chart has much larger values, increase its physical size rather than changing its scale.

**Exceptions / limits.** The practical limit depends on page and font size. All small multiples must use the identical scale.

**Audit.** Do all small multiples share type and scale? Is the count within a readable limit?

**See also.** EX 2.4, CH 4.1.

**Source.** p. 153–154.

### CO 5.2 — Show related charts on one page

**Requirement.** Place related charts that cover different topics or measures on one page when the relationship is useful.

**Why.** Related charts offer high data density and comparability, but only if the relationship is real and the notation is uniform.

**Apply.** Use a consistent notation, clear terms, and an understandable scaling. Where different scales are unavoidable for the same unit, use scaling markers. Arrange the charts in a meaningful layout. For root-cause analysis, use a ratio tree: break a ratio into its components, usually left to right, with individual charts of preferably identical size arranged in a tree.

**Exceptions / limits.** Do not arrange unrelated charts on one page merely to fill space. Different measures often require different scales; mark them.

**Audit.** Is there a real relationship between the charts, and is the scaling clear?

**See also.** EX 5.1, CO 5.1, UN 5.2.

**Source.** p. 155.

### CO 5.3 — Show chart-table combinations

**Requirement.** Combine a separate chart with a table on the same page when each displays supplementary data.

**Why.** A chart and a table can serve different purposes: the chart shows the pattern, the table provides precise values or context.

**Apply.** Add the chart next to the table (or vice versa) and make the supplementary relationship clear. Do not duplicate the same numbers in both.

**Exceptions / limits.** This is different from embedding chart components in a table (CO 4.4). A table that merely lists the numbers already shown in a chart is usually superfluous.

**Audit.** Does each visual add information the other does not?

**See also.** CO 4.4, EX 1.2.

**Source.** p. 156.

### CO 5.4 — Show charts and tables in text pages

**Requirement.** Embed charts and tables in the text of a written report to help the audience understand the key message.

**Why.** A visual placed next to the sentence it supports is easier to use than one separated from the argument.

**Apply.** Position the visual close to the phrase carrying the key message. Give text pages a title like other pages, and give every embedded visual a title and, if possible, a key message.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Is each visual close to the text it supports and properly titled?

**See also.** SA 3.2, CO 5.3.

**Source.** p. 156.
