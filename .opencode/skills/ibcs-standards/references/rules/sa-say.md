# SAY — Convey Message

SAY covers all aspects of conveying messages to the audience. Reports, presentations, and dashboards, both as a whole and within their individual components, should communicate specific information and say something to the audience. Messages can be detections, explanations, clarifications, recommendations, and other statements.

This chapter addresses introducing, delivering, supporting, and summarizing key messages in relation to the goals of both senders and receivers.

**Source:** IBCS v2.0, pages 78–86.

**Key message template:** `<Subject> <verb> <magnitude> <comparison> <time>, <consequence>.`

Example: “Net sales grew 8.4% to 124 mEUR in 2026, 3.1 pp above plan, driven by Wholesale.”

## SA 1 — Know objectives

Good message-conveying reporting successfully achieves both the goals of the author and of the audience.

### SA 1.1 — Know own goals

**Requirement.** Do not start reporting without a clear vision of what the reporting is to achieve.

**Why.** The content, structure, and level of detail depend on whether the goal is to inform, to make the audience understand a problem, or to obtain a decision and cause action.

**Apply.** State the goal before designing. The least goal is to inform about an interesting detection; a higher goal is to make the audience understand a problem by explaining it; the ultimate goal is to obtain a decision on a suggestion and cause corresponding actions. Choose the message type and evidence accordingly.

**Exceptions / limits.** A single page may serve more than one goal, but the primary goal should be explicit.

**Audit.** Is the reporting's goal explicit, and does the content serve it?

**See also.** SA 1.2, SA 3.1, SA 5.2.

**Source.** p. 78.

### SA 1.2 — Know target audience

**Requirement.** Know the target audience and design the reporting to answer their questions.

**Why.** The audience's functions, positions, knowledge, experience, attitudes, behavior, worries, and cultural backgrounds determine what they need and how they will read the message.

**Apply.** Identify the audience's goals, preferences, and expectations. Determine whether they want to be informed about a detection, need an explanation of a problem, or are willing and able to make a decision. Consider who might disagree with the key message and why.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Does the page address the audience's questions and level of knowledge?

**See also.** SA 1.1, SA 2.1.

**Source.** p. 78–79.

## SA 2 — Introduce message

The audience appreciates an introduction that maps the actual situation, explains the problem, and raises a question that focuses on the key message. This is the situation → complication → question → answer sequence.

### SA 2.1 — Map situation

**Requirement.** Compile and present the facts that map the situation before describing the problem.

**Why.** A shared, accepted picture of the facts creates a foundation for the audience to accept the problem and the message.

**Apply.** Cover all relevant aspects and seek consensus on the facts. Present facts and goals already known to the audience. Start with a positive or widely accepted description of the situation to prevent early contradictions.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Does the introduction establish the relevant facts before introducing the complication?

**See also.** SA 2.2, ST 1.

**Source.** p. 79.

### SA 2.2 — Explain problem

**Requirement.** After mapping the situation, introduce the challenge or complication that affects the audience.

**Why.** The complication makes the audience aware of an interesting, critical, or dangerous problem and creates the need for an answer.

**Apply.** State the complication clearly and show its relevance to the audience. Do not jump from facts to a solution without making the problem explicit.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Is the complication explicit, relevant, and clearly separated from the situation?

**See also.** SA 2.3, SA 3.1.

**Source.** p. 79–80.

### SA 2.3 — Raise question

**Requirement.** Raise the relevant question from the audience's perspective: how to solve the complication in the situation described.

**Why.** The question at the beginning of the reporting leads naturally to the key message, which is the answer.

**Apply.** Phrase the question in the audience's words, then answer it with the key message. Keep the question and answer aligned.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Does the introduction end with a question that the key message answers?

**See also.** SA 3.1, SA 3.2.

**Source.** p. 80.

## SA 3 — Deliver message

Delivering the key message means answering the question asked at the end of the introduction. Key messages detect, explain, or suggest aspects that will be explored in more depth in the subsequent reporting.

### SA 3.1 — Detect, explain, or suggest

**Requirement.** State key messages in complete sentences. Decide whether the message is a detection, an explanation, or a suggestion, and formulate it accordingly.

**Why.** Complete sentences are understood; fragments leave the audience to infer the point. Detections can be checked as true or false and should be as precise as possible. Explanations and suggestions add more value than a bare detection.

**Apply.** Use the key message template. A detection states what happened and the relevant comparison; an explanation gives reasons; a suggestion recommends a decision or action. Include the magnitude, comparison, time, and consequence where relevant.

**Exceptions / limits.** A page may contain a detection followed by an explanation in annotations, but the key message itself should be one clear statement.

**Audit.** Is the key message a complete sentence, and is it clearly a detection, explanation, or suggestion?

**See also.** SA 3.2, SA 4.1, SA 5.2.

**Source.** p. 80–81.

### SA 3.2 — Say message first

**Requirement.** Put the overall key message first, then prove it. Every report, presentation, dashboard, page, or exhibit can be summarized with a clear overall key message.

**Why.** The audience finds it harder to follow the storyline when the key message comes at the end. Leading with the answer respects the audience's time and frames the evidence.

**Apply.** Write the key message as the first text element the audience reads, then support it with visuals, evidence, and annotations. Position and design it consistently (UN 2.1).

**Exceptions / limits.** Exercise caution in presentations (not written reports) involving negative, unexpected, or unpleasant messages, such as layoffs, or in cultural contexts where directness may be perceived as impolite. In those cases, build the context before stating the message, but do not bury it.

**Audit.** Does the key message appear before the evidence? If it is delayed, is there a documented cultural or sensitive-message reason?

**See also.** SA 3.1, SA 4.1, UN 2.1.

**Source.** p. 82.

## SA 4 — Support message

Supporting the message covers technical and practical aspects of message conveyance.

### SA 4.1 — Provide evidence

**Requirement.** Substantiate the key message with facts and figures.

**Why.** Evidence proves the message and protects it from being dismissed as opinion. A presentation slide should explain or prove the speaker's message by itself rather than depend on the speaker to explain the visual.

**Apply.** Use charts, tables, and images that directly support the message. Add spoken or written sentences that connect the evidence to the claim. Remove evidence that does not bear on the message.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Does the page provide specific evidence for its key message?

**See also.** SA 4.2, SA 4.4, UN 2.5.

**Source.** p. 82.

### SA 4.2 — Use precise words

**Requirement.** Use unambiguous language and precise words.

**Why.** Vague words such as “relevant” or “significant” (in common speech, not as a statistical term) lead to misinterpretation and misunderstanding. Facts and figures prevent this.

**Apply.** Replace vague qualifiers with quantities, comparisons, periods, and sources. Use the organization's unified terminology, numbers, units, and dates (UN 1.1, UN 1.2).

**Exceptions / limits.** “Significant” may be used in its statistical sense when the statistical test and threshold are stated.

**Audit.** Can every qualitative claim be tied to a quantity, comparison, or source?

**See also.** SA 4.1, UN 1.1, UN 1.2.

**Source.** p. 83.

### SA 4.3 — Highlight message

**Requirement.** Visually highlight the key message in the visuals: charts, tables, graphs, and pictures.

**Why.** Highlighting the important facts or interesting details enhances understanding and reduces the time needed to grasp a complex situation. Visuals and pages without highlighting markers tend to be statistics rather than messages.

**Apply.** Underline the most important facts or emphasize interesting details with the consistent highlighting markers in UN 5.1. Highlight only what supports the key message; do not highlight everything.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. A page without a message may legitimately have no highlight, but then it is a statistics page, not a message page.

**Audit.** Does the visual highlight the evidence for the key message? Is the highlighting consistent?

**See also.** UN 5.1, SA 4.5.

**Source.** p. 83–84.

### SA 4.4 — Name sources

**Requirement.** Name the sources of the material presented.

**Why.** Naming sources increases credibility and allows the audience to verify the data.

**Apply.** Put source names, definitions, and retrieval dates in footnotes at the bottom of the page. Projected slides may exclude them, but written reports, handouts, and dashboards must include them.

**Exceptions / limits.** Slides shown during a presentation may omit source footnotes if the speaker provides them; the written version must still include them.

**Audit.** Are sources named and retrievable for every material claim?

**See also.** SA 4.1, UN 2.5.

**Source.** p. 84.

### SA 4.5 — Link annotations

**Requirement.** Use annotations in written reports, handouts, and dashboards to add explanations, conclusions, and similar statements, and link each annotation to the part of the page it explains.

**Why.** Linked annotations add context without forcing the reader to guess which element they refer to. Slides shown during presentations typically do not require annotations because the speaker provides them.

**Apply.** Number annotations related to specific words, numbers, or visual components and link them to those parts. Post numbered annotations in text boxes in free areas of the page. Use the consistent annotation design and references from UN 2.4 and UN 5.1.

**Exceptions / limits.** Presentation slides may omit annotations when the speaker supplies the explanation; written reports, handouts, and dashboards must include them.

**Audit.** Is every annotation linked to the element it explains, and is the design consistent?

**See also.** UN 2.4, UN 5.1, SA 4.3.

**Source.** p. 84–85.

## SA 5 — Summarize message

Conclude a presentation with the overall key message, including next steps and a clarification of the consequences and implications.

### SA 5.1 — Repeat message

**Requirement.** At the end of a presentation, briefly sum up the key message one last time, in one sentence if possible. Do not end with “Thank you for your attention.”

**Why.** Repetition brings the presentation full circle and lets the audience check the quality of the storyline. At the end of a successful presentation, the audience will thank the presenters for the information.

**Apply.** Repeat the key message from the beginning and connect it to the next steps. In written reports and dashboards, do not repeat the message mechanically; readers can browse back to the summary at the beginning.

**Exceptions / limits.** The repetition rule applies to presentations. Written reports and dashboards generally do not need a closing repetition.

**Audit.** Does the presentation close with the key message rather than a generic thank-you? Does the written report avoid unnecessary repetition?

**See also.** SA 3.2, SA 5.2.

**Source.** p. 85.

### SA 5.2 — Explain consequences

**Requirement.** Conclude the reporting by presenting proposals for decisions to be taken and an explanation of their consequences and implications.

**Why.** The real objective of a presentation is to convince the audience of both the key message and the suggested next steps.

**Apply.** State the proposed decision or action, explain its consequences and implications, and make the next step clear. Distinguish consequences that are certain from those that are expected or conditional.

**Exceptions / limits.** No source exception. Limit: an informational report may have no decision proposal, but it should still state what follows from the findings.

**Audit.** Does the conclusion say what should happen next and what it would mean?

**See also.** SA 1.1, SA 5.1.

**Source.** p. 86.
