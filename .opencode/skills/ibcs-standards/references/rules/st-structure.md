# STRUCTURE — Organize Content

STRUCTURE covers all aspects of organizing reporting content. Organizing content means that it follows a logical structure forming a convincing storyline.

This chapter covers using consistent elements, building non-overlapping elements, building collectively exhaustive elements, building hierarchical structures, and visualizing structure properly.

**Source:** IBCS v2.0, pages 87–95.

## ST 1 — Use consistent elements

Listings and groupings used to organize content in charts, tables, and texts should contain consistent elements only. This applies to items, statements, wordings, and the appearance of symbols and pictures.

### ST 1.1 — Use consistent items

**Requirement.** Items in a group should be of the same type.

**Why.** A group of unlike items breaks the reader's expectation and makes the grouping harder to perceive.

**Apply.** Group like with like: flags with flags, products with products, countries with countries. Do not mix categories of different kinds in one list unless the mixture itself is the point and is explained.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Are all items in the group of the same kind?

**See also.** ST 1.2, ST 1.4.

**Source.** p. 87.

### ST 1.2 — Use consistent types of statements

**Requirement.** All statements in a list should be of the same type.

**Why.** A list of statements is easier to understand when every statement performs the same function.

**Apply.** Do not mix detections, explanations, and suggestions in one list. If the list is a set of suggestions, every item should be a suggestion.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. A deliberate mixed list should be split into labeled groups.

**Audit.** Do all statements in the list have the same type?

**See also.** ST 1.3, SA 3.1.

**Source.** p. 87–88.

### ST 1.3 — Use consistent wording

**Requirement.** Structure phrases, especially in lists, in a grammatically consistent manner.

**Why.** Parallel wording lets the reader scan the list faster and compare items without re-parsing each one.

**Apply.** Use the same grammatical pattern for every item: for example, an imperative verb paired with a noun throughout a list of suggestions. Avoid switching between imperative forms and verbal nouns.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Do the items share the same grammatical pattern?

**See also.** ST 1.2, UN 1.1.

**Source.** p. 88.

### ST 1.4 — Use consistent visualizations

**Requirement.** Symbols and pictures that are uniform in layout, color, form, and font should be used consistently, especially in lists.

**Why.** Consistent visual treatment speeds comprehension and lets the reader recognize structure.

**Apply.** Use the same notation for the same structural dimension throughout the report, following UN 3.4. Keep symbols, colors, and forms stable across pages.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Are the same visual forms used for the same content?

**See also.** ST 1.1, UN 3.4.

**Source.** p. 88–89.

## ST 2 — Build non-overlapping elements

Elements belonging to a group should not overlap; they should be mutually exclusive. This concerns reporting structures, business measures, and structure dimensions.

### ST 2.1 — Build non-overlapping reporting structures

**Requirement.** Structure the reporting so that parts, chapters, sections, and paragraphs do not overlap and do not cover the same aspects.

**Why.** Overlapping chapters force the reader to reconcile repeated content and obscure the storyline.

**Apply.** Define the scope of each part and check for shared topics. Separate related but distinct aspects even when they influence each other. For example, do not create one chapter for “expenses and costs” and another for “cost of goods sold” if the second is a subset of the first.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. A relationship between topics is not necessarily an overlap.

**Audit.** Do any two parts of the reporting cover the same aspect?

**See also.** ST 2.2, ST 2.3.

**Source.** p. 89–90.

### ST 2.2 — Build non-overlapping business measures

**Requirement.** Structure a group of business measures in lists or calculations so that measures on one hierarchical level are disjoint or mutually exclusive.

**Why.** Overlapping measures double-count and make a calculation scheme impossible to interpret.

**Apply.** Check each level of the calculation. For example, material costs and cost of goods sold overlap when material costs are a component of cost of goods sold; depreciation and fixed costs overlap when depreciation is already included in fixed costs. Move components to the level where they belong.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. A measure may legitimately appear at different levels if it is labeled as a subtotal or component.

**Audit.** Do any two measures on the same level overlap or double-count?

**See also.** ST 2.1, ST 3.1.

**Source.** p. 90.

### ST 2.3 — Build non-overlapping structure dimensions

**Requirement.** Categories of a structure dimension should be disjoint or mutually exclusive.

**Why.** Overlapping categories make totals meaningless and comparisons ambiguous.

**Apply.** Check the category set. For example, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, and Finland overlap with the category “Nordics” if Nordics is presented at the same level as those countries. Put the countries under Nordics or use one level, not both.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. Hierarchical levels may contain parents and children if the levels are clearly distinguished.

**Audit.** Do any categories at the same level overlap?

**See also.** ST 2.1, ST 4.2.

**Source.** p. 90–91.

## ST 3 — Build collectively exhaustive elements

A list of elements is exhaustive when it covers all aspects of a superordinate topic. For example, dividing Europe into Germany, Austria, Switzerland, and Belgium is not exhaustive because other countries also belong to Europe. Structures with mutually exclusive (ME) and collectively exhaustive (CE) elements are MECE structures.

### ST 3.1 — Build exhaustive arguments

**Requirement.** Include all important arguments relating to the question.

**Why.** If an important argument is left out, the answer will not be convincing.

**Apply.** Enumerate the relevant options or arguments and check for omissions. For example, a product/location matrix that covers “old products, old location,” “new products, old location,” and “new products, new location” but omits “old products, new location” is not exhaustive.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. Immaterial arguments may be grouped and labeled as such.

**Audit.** Is any important argument or option missing?

**See also.** ST 3.2, ST 2.2.

**Source.** p. 91.

### ST 3.2 — Build exhaustive structures

**Requirement.** Categories of structure dimensions presented in charts and tables should be exhaustive, adding up to 100% of the relevant total.

**Why.** A structure that does not cover the whole cannot support a complete comparison or a reliable total.

**Apply.** Include all categories or add a remainder category (“Rest of…”) for the smallest or least important ones. Use consistent notation for remainder categories in charts and tables.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. A remainder category is appropriate when listing every category would make the visual unreadable.

**Audit.** Do the categories cover the whole? If not, is a remainder category present and labeled?

**See also.** ST 3.1, EX 1.1, EX 1.2.

**Source.** p. 91–92.

## ST 4 — Build hierarchical structures

Give the reporting a hierarchical structure whenever possible; this results in faster comprehension and simplified searching.

### ST 4.1 — Use deductive reasoning

**Requirement.** Use deductive reasoning (logical flow) to build a hierarchical structure that answers “why” after the key message.

**Why.** A logical flow takes the audience from accepted premises to a conclusion and can be persuasive in controversial discussions or when demonstrating a need for action.

**Apply.** Start with a statement, continue with a comment, and resolve with a conclusion that culminates in the message. For example: “All men are mortal” → “Socrates is a man” → “Socrates is mortal.” Keep every premise defensible.

**Exceptions / limits.** Deductive reasoning forces the audience to reproduce the deduction, and the whole argument can collapse if any statement is questionable. Use it when the premises are strong; otherwise use an inductive group.

**Audit.** Does the argument follow a valid deduction, and are the premises accepted by the audience?

**See also.** ST 4.2, SA 3.2.

**Source.** p. 92–93.

### ST 4.2 — Use inductive reasoning

**Requirement.** Use inductive reasoning (logical group) to build a hierarchical structure from homogeneous, non-overlapping, collectively exhaustive arguments that culminate in a key message.

**Why.** A well-formed logical group satisfies the audience's need for an easily comprehensible structure and produces a powerful argument.

**Apply.** Group the arguments by a shared characteristic, check that they do not overlap, and check that together they cover the issue. State the key message as the conclusion of the group.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0. If the group is not exhaustive, add the missing argument or a labeled remainder.

**Audit.** Are the arguments homogeneous, mutually exclusive, and collectively exhaustive?

**See also.** ST 2.2, ST 3.1, ST 4.1.

**Source.** p. 93.

## ST 5 — Visualize structure

After organizing the arguments hierarchically, visualize the structure so the storyline becomes transparent.

### ST 5.1 — Visualize structure in reporting

**Requirement.** Underscore the logical structure of the reporting with visual aids such as outlines, dashboards, and summaries.

**Why.** Visual structure helps the reader see where they are in the storyline and how the parts relate.

**Apply.** Use consistent section markers, outlines, tabs, or summary pages that mirror the hierarchy. For example, binder tabs can show the parts of a written report.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Does the page or report make its logical structure visible?

**See also.** ST 5.2, ST 5.3.

**Source.** p. 93–94.

### ST 5.2 — Visualize structure in tables

**Requirement.** Design tables so that their structure can be recognized in both columns and rows.

**Why.** A table with visible hierarchy can be read at multiple levels without losing the relationship between detail and total.

**Apply.** Use indentation, gaps, lines, bold totals, and consistent row/column types to show levels. For example, show cities at the base level, regions as the first summary, and the country as the second summary, with parents placed consistently.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Can the reader see the hierarchy in both directions?

**See also.** ST 5.1, EX 1.2.

**Source.** p. 94.

### ST 5.3 — Visualize structure in notes

**Requirement.** Show the structure of notes clearly.

**Why.** Notes are easier to understand when their internal hierarchy and relationships are visible.

**Apply.** Use indentation, numbering, grouping, and consistent annotation design. Link notes to the content they explain with the annotation references from UN 2.4 and UN 5.1.

**Exceptions / limits.** None stated in v2.0.

**Audit.** Is the structure of the note visible, and are its parts linked to the content?

**See also.** ST 5.1, UN 2.4, UN 5.1.

**Source.** p. 94–95.
