# IBCS Framework

## Purpose and source

This reference is part of the closed-book IBCS skill. It gives the mental model, definitions, and page anatomy needed to apply the standards without opening the source PDF.

**Source:** *International Business Communication Standards (IBCS) Version 2.0*, Rolf Hichert and Jürgen Faisst, IBCS Media, 2026-07-02, 169 pages, CC BY-SA 4.0.

**Scope:** 35 top-level rules and 105 sub-rules. Notation is aligned with ISO 24896; composition is IBCS guidance. This skill is a practical substitute, not a verbatim reproduction of ISO 24896 and not a certification authority.

## Notation vs composition

| Layer | Question | Principles | What it governs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Notation** | Does the visual mean what it appears to mean? | SIMPLIFY, UNIFY, CHECK | Clutter, terminology, dimensions, scenarios, variances, scales, integrity |
| **Composition** | Does the page help the audience decide? | SAY, STRUCTURE, EXPRESS, CONDENSE | Message, storyline, chart/table choice, information density |

Notation without composition produces clean but pointless pages. Composition without notation produces persuasive but unreliable pages. Apply both.

The source presents notation first (SIMPLIFY, UNIFY, CHECK) and composition second (SAY, STRUCTURE, EXPRESS, CONDENSE). The SUCCESS acronym lists the principles in a different order; the table below follows the acronym and names the document section.

## Definitions

**Business reporting.** The sharing of business information to support analysis and decision-making. Reports consist of one or more report pages, which contain visuals made of visual components.

**Report.** A compilation of information displayed on one or multiple pages or screens: written reports, presentations, and dashboards. The term covers static and interactive reporting.

**Report page.** A single sheet of a written report, a single slide of a presentation, or a single screen of an interactive analytic system. Page size and grid determine the layout.

**Visual.** A communication object placed on a page, such as a chart, table, or image. A visual represents an analytic view and can stand alone with or without a message.

**Visual component.** A part of a visual. Specific components include bars, columns, lines, legends, labels, and axes. General components include titles, footnotes, key messages, and annotations; they are not used to construct the visual but are necessary to understand it.

**Dimension.** A set of one characteristic area consisting of individual elements that categorize data points, such as measures, scenarios, periods, or regions.

**Category.** An element of a dimension.

**Measure.** A category of a dimension with a quantitative metric used to evaluate and manage performance. Measures can be financial or non-financial, such as return on investment or employee turnover rate.

**Scenario.** A category of a dimension representing a data layer or version to be compared, such as Actual, Budget, or Forecast. External benchmarks can also be treated as scenarios.

**Period.** A category of a time dimension representing a time frame such as a day, week, month, quarter, or year. Flow measures such as sales refer to the entire period; stock measures such as inventory are captured at the beginning or end.

**Structure dimension.** Any dimension other than measures, scenarios, and periods. Structure dimensions typically represent hierarchical structures such as regions, products, or organizational units.

**Notation manual.** A comprehensive document with detailed instructions and standards for visual components: color, typography, formatting, and other styles. It ensures consistency across reports and is typically developed by reporting and design teams.

## ISO 24896 relationship

IBCS v2.0 aligns its notation guidance with ISO 24896 “Notation for business reporting.” ISO 24896 defines normative requirements for notation; IBCS provides explanations, design rules, examples, and extensions for practical application. The composition principles (SAY, STRUCTURE, EXPRESS, CONDENSE) are IBCS guidance and are not a reproduction of ISO text.

This skill does not reproduce ISO 24896. For formal certification, legal review, or contractual compliance, verify against the ISO standard and the current IBCS source.

## Page anatomy

A complete report page usually contains, from top to bottom:

1. **Key message.** One complete sentence stating the main finding, with the relevant number, direction, and comparison. It is the first thing the reader should see.
2. **Page title — line 1: who.** The reporting unit: legal entity, organizational unit, line of business, project, or filter.
3. **Page title — line 2: what.** The business measure(s), including units and any analytical annotation such as sorting or indexing.
4. **Page title — line 3: when.** The time period(s), scenario(s), and variance(s), using the standard abbreviations.
5. **Subtitle.** Only when a page has multiple visuals; identifies the visual or page segment and the categories that differ from visual to visual.
6. **Visual.** The chart or table, with its axes, series, and data labels.
7. **Labels and legends.** Integrated where possible, horizontal, positioned consistently.
8. **Annotations.** Explanatory text linked to the relevant visual component by annotation references.
9. **Footnotes and source.** Definitions, sources, retrieval dates, and other credibility details at the bottom.

Titles are purely descriptive. They must not contain interpretations, conclusions, or propositions; those belong in the key message and annotations.

## How to use this skill

Use the create workflow when designing a page:

1. Frame the decision and audience.
2. Write the key message.
3. Structure the argument MECE.
4. Choose the visual.
5. Encode meaning.
6. Compose the page.
7. Verify integrity.

Use the review workflow when auditing an existing page:

1. Read as a decision-maker.
2. Integrity pass (critical).
3. Semantic pass (major).
4. Composition pass (major).
5. Clutter pass (minor).
6. Report findings with severity, rule ID, evidence, and fix.
